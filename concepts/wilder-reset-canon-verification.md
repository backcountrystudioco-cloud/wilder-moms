# Wilder Reset — Verification Against the Urban Design & Behavioral Canon

> A line-by-line audit of the Reset brief against the foundational works the parent asked me to consult. The brief being verified lives at `concepts/wilder-reset-brief.md`. This file is read-only commentary. Nothing was modified in the brief.

---

## 0. Bottom line

The five slots are well-chosen at the level of metaphor and most of them have a strong, defensible lineage in the canon. The architecture is sound. The weaknesses are **second-order**: a missing community dimension in Arrival, a missing materials dimension in Play, a missing physical/place dimension in Boundary, a missing other-adults dimension in Mom, and a missing celebration/feedback dimension across the whole year. The Q3 "Rhythm" quarter is the weakest quarter canonically — it leans on behavioral science more than urban design, and even there it underweights Whyte's *observation* principle. The gendered "Mom" framing is the one place where the brief visibly departs from the canon's more universal caregiver language.

Five concrete recommendations at the end of this document. Each cites the source.

## B. The 5 move slots — verified against the canon

Each slot in the brief gets a verdict. "Anchor" = the strongest primary citation. "Secondary" = the 1–2 patterns that deepen it. "Gap" = where the slot underweights something the canon says it should be doing.

### B.1 ARRIVAL — "Kids go directly to the backyard before entering the house."

- **Anchor**: Alexander #160 *Building Edge* and #168 *Outdoor Room* (1977). The building edge is a "transition zone" that mediates between inside and outside. The Reset's "go to the backyard first" is essentially converting the home's primary entry into a half-outdoor room before the indoor threshold.
- **Secondary**: Gehl's "soft edge" criterion. *Cities for People* treats the threshold between building and street as a *gradient*, not a wall; a soft edge keeps people engaged with the life on the other side (Gehl Institute, *Quality Criteria*, 2017).
- **Secondary**: Whyte's *Social Life of Small Urban Spaces* principle that "people attract people" — the entry to a space is the single most important place where a person decides whether to stay.
- **Gap (the canon says more)**: Jacobs's "eyes on the street" is not really about the family themselves; it's about **neighbors' visibility of the family**. The Reset's arrival move is inward-looking ("kids go to the backyard"). It misses the half of "eyes on the street" that is the *neighbor at the window, the regular dog-walker, the other parent on the stoop*. Sampson, Raudenbush & Earls (*Science* 277, 1997) is the empirical proof that visibility and shared willingness to intervene are what make a neighborhood work for children.
- **Gap (the canon says more)**: Allan Jacobs's *Great Streets* and Alexander #165 *Openness to the Street* are about a building *opening* outward. The brief's arrival move is about the family *entering* — the orientation is wrong. A canon-aligned arrival move should also point outward (e.g., "wave to the neighbor," "stop at the front stoop for 30 seconds") rather than purely back toward the private yard.
- **Verdict**: Slot is *correct in spirit, directionally inverted*. The Reset treats arrival as a private act. The canon treats it as a public-private threshold. This is the slot to fix first.

### B.2 CHANGE — "Move shoes, jackets, and outdoor clothes from the bedroom closets to the garage entry."

- **Anchor**: Alexander #61 *Small Public Outdoor Room* (1977). A small, well-defined transition space that mediates a "change" — the entry in this case — and is detailed enough that people use it without thinking.
- **Secondary**: Project for Public Spaces' "choice" criterion: a great place gives people a *small* range of clear options. The change slot, properly executed, gives the kids a one-handed path (shoes here, jacket here, into the yard) that is itself a choice architecture.
- **Gap (the canon says more)**: Alexander's threshold is a *ritual space*, not a logistics zone. The brief frames change as a *move the shoes* problem. The canon (Gehl's "soft edges" again, and Alexander #179 *Alcoves* and #150 *A Place to Wait*) treats the threshold as a *small architectural room* with its own light, its own bench, its own micro-climate. The Reset underdesigns this — it tells you to move objects, not to make a room.
- **Gap (the canon says more)**: The change move is also a *hand-off* between adult and child. Whyte's triangulation principle says the value of a small intervention often comes from the *unscripted third thing* that happens at it. A well-designed entry gives a kid 30 seconds of autonomy (their own bench, their own hook, their own drawer) that is the *first* independence of the day. The brief mentions autonomy in Q2 but the change slot is where it actually starts.
- **Verdict**: Slot is *logistically right, experientially thin*. A pure logistics move is closer to Marie Kondo than to *Pattern Language*.

### B.3 PLAY — "Put the scooters where the kids can access them without asking."

- **Anchor**: Nicholson's Theory of Loose Parts (1971). "In any environment, both the degree of inventiveness and creativity, and the possibility of discovery, are directly proportional to the number and kind of variables in it." The brief gestures at this (kids can access the things) but does not actually name it.
- **Secondary**: Alexander #72 *Local Sports*, #83 *Mastery* (the latter is the principle that children need a *gradient* of challenge to develop competence, not a single fixed play object).
- **Secondary**: Sandseter's six categories of risky play (2009/2015): great heights, high speed, dangerous tools, dangerous elements, rough-and-tumble, disappear/get lost. A "where are the scooters" move is one affordance in the "high speed" category. The brief has no affordance in the other five.
- **Gap (the canon says the slot misses the materials question)**: This is the biggest miss. Nicholson's loose parts are *not* access to existing things; they are *variable, manipulable, combinable materials*. Sticks, cardboard boxes, tarps, rope, planks, water. A scooter is a *fixed affordance* — it does what it does, the same way every time. The canon's play move would say "what materials do you have that kids can combine in new ways?" The brief treats Play as an access problem (no permission needed) when the canon treats Play as a variables problem (more variables, more invention).
- **Gap (the canon says the slot misses the social question)**: Whyte's *Social Life of Small Urban Spaces* repeatedly shows that what makes a small space "work" is *what people do with each other there*, not the equipment. The brief's Play move is solo. A canon-aligned Play move would specify what *the other child, the neighbor, the dog, the parent on the porch* does in the play space. This is also directly tied to UNICEF CFCI domain "Play and Leisure" and the principle that children need *peers*, not just objects.
- **Verdict**: Slot is the weakest of the five. It captures *access* but not *affordances* or *materials* or *social play*. This is the slot to fix second.

### B.4 BOUNDARY — "Screens don't become available until after dinner."

- **Anchor**: Gehl's "protection" criterion (one of the 12 quality criteria). A great place protects people from the negative sides of urban life — noise, fast traffic, intrusion — so they can do the positive things. A screen boundary is a *temporal* protection, not a spatial one, but the structure is the same: define the inside, protect it, let the rest happen.
- **Secondary**: Alexander #180 *Window Place* and #179 *Alcoves* — the *inviting* boundary. A boundary that says "you can sit here, you can watch from here, you can pass through" is a *permeable* boundary. The brief's "no screens until after dinner" is a *hard* boundary. The canon prefers *soft* boundaries that invite a particular behavior rather than forbid another.
- **Gap (the canon says the boundary should be playful, not forbidding)**: Brussoni et al. (2015, PubMed 26062038) explicitly distinguish *good risk* from *bad risk*. The same logic applies to boundaries: a boundary that *invites* a specific outside behavior (a certain chair, a certain hour, a certain snack) outperforms a boundary that *forbids* a default (no screens, no exceptions). The brief leans into the *forbidding* frame.
- **Gap (the canon says the boundary should also be physical)**: Gehl's "soft edge" again. The brief's boundary is a *rule* (a clock-based prohibition). The canon's boundary is also a *place* (the screen-charging station lives in a specific drawer; the "outside" default is the front path, the stoop, the park three doors down). The Reset underdesigns the *where* of the boundary.
- **Gap (the canon says the boundary is a learning moment, not a rule)**: Alexander #83 *Mastery* treats the boundary as a *gradient* the child learns to navigate. Whyte's "triangulation" works the same way — the boundary creates a *third thing* (a small social interaction) that wouldn't happen without it. The brief's boundary, as written, is a one-line prohibition; the canon would ask the boundary to *teach* something.
- **Verdict**: Slot is *well-named but rule-bound*. The canon prefers boundaries-as-invitations. This is the slot to fix third.

### B.5 MOM — "You don't have to participate. Your job is to make the transition possible."

- **Anchor**: Jacobs's "eyes on the street" (1961) as a *civic* practice — and yes, this is genuinely the right citation. The parent on the stoop, the neighbor at the window, the adult whose job is to *be there* is one of the most replicated findings in urban sociology, formalized by Sampson et al. (1997) as *collective efficacy*.
- **Secondary**: Alexander #37 *House Cluster*. The pattern is explicit: a group of houses forms a *cluster* organized around a shared outdoor space, and the cluster is held together by the *small social contracts* its members make. The parent is the *organizer of the cluster* — at the family scale.
- **Gap (the canon says the Mom slot is missing the other adults)**: This is the second-biggest miss. Alexander #38 *Marriage Family* and #37 *House Cluster* are *not* about a single adult carrying the cognitive load. The cluster pattern is *multiple* adults, multiple households, with overlapping obligations. The brief's "Mom" slot is *one* adult doing *all* the work. Hartz-Karp's deliberative-design work and UNICEF CFCI's "Voice" domain both explicitly call for *children and other caregivers as co-designers*. The brief's slot names only the primary caregiver.
- **Gap (the canon says the slot is also about the child's growing role)**: UNICEF CFCI domain "Participation" and Hartz-Karp both call for the *child* to be a participant in shaping the routine, not just a recipient of it. The brief's slot is "the adult's role"; the canon would add "the kid's growing role" — the slot should expand as the year progresses.
- **Verdict**: Slot is *correct in principle, too narrow in practice*. The Reset treats the family as one adult + N children. The canon treats the family as a *cluster* — adults, children, neighbors, grandparents, other parents, the regular dog-walker.

### B.6 Cross-slot observation

The five slots are presented in a sequence (arrival → change → play → boundary → mom) that mirrors a *spatial gradient* (outside → threshold → inside → across-the-day → the adult's role). This is exactly the way Gehl organizes his 12 quality criteria — from the outside-in, from the physical to the social. The Reset inherits Gehl's ordering almost by accident; this is a strength worth naming.

The five slots are also *collectively* the *house cluster* pattern, broken into its component moves. That is the strongest single citation for the whole structure: this is *A Pattern Language* #37, decomposed.

## C. The 6 Wilder dimensions — verified against frameworks

Each dimension in the existing Wilder Index gets a verdict. Brief: every dimension is well-supported; a few have stronger empirical lineages than others.

### C.1 belonging

- **Frameworks that justify**:
  - Jacobs, "eyes on the street" (1961, ch. 3) — the *informal* social control and the *casual* public contact that make a block a block.
  - Alexander #37 *House Cluster* (1977) — the spatial form of belonging at the scale of 8–12 households.
  - Sampson, Raudenbush & Earls, "Neighborhoods and Violent Crime: A Multilevel Study of Collective Efficacy," *Science* 277 (1997): 918–924 — the empirical validation that *cohesion + shared willingness to intervene* predict lower violence and higher child wellbeing.
- **Verdict**: Strongest dimension. The three citations are mutually reinforcing (Jacobs the theory, Alexander the form, Sampson the empirics). No gap.

### C.2 nearby-nature

- **Frameworks that justify**:
  - Richard Louv, *Last Child in the Woods* (2005) — "nature-deficit disorder" as a working name for the condition the dimension is meant to measure.
  - E. O. Wilson, *Biophilia* (1984); Stephen R. Kellert (ed.), *The Biophilia Hypothesis* (1993) — the *innate* human affinity for living systems, with Kellert's six biophilic values (biophilia as a *measurable* affinity, not a metaphor).
  - Kaplan & Kaplan, *The Experience of Nature* (1989) — preference matrix showing that humans prefer savanna-like settings with the *prospect-refuge* signature.
  - Recent (2024) corroboration: a randomized trial in *Journal of Environmental Psychology* (Sciencedirect S0272494424001567) directly links nature exposure to pro-environmental behavior in children.
- **Verdict**: Strong. Louv is the popular source, Kellert is the rigorous source, and the 2024 evidence base continues to grow. The dimension name "nearby-nature" is itself Wolf's framing from the *nearby nature* literature — cite that explicitly.

### C.3 wonder

- **Frameworks that justify**:
  - Kaplan & Kaplan (1989) preference matrix — environments that are *coherent*, *complex*, and *legible* generate sustained fascination, not just initial liking.
  - Shari Tishman, *Slow Looking* (Routledge, 2018) — wonder as a *practice* of attention, not a passive state. Tishman explicitly links slow looking to cognitive development in children.
  - Arnold Berleant, *The Aesthetics of Environment* (1992) and continuing — *aesthetic engagement* as a mode of inhabiting the world, which is what wonder is, formally.
- **Gap**: The dimension is *philosophically* supported but the empirical literature is thinner than for nearby-nature or restoration. The Reset should pair "wonder" with a Tishman-style *practice* (a "slow looking" prompt) rather than treating it as something that just happens.
- **Verdict**: Well-grounded; under-developed as a *practice*.

### C.4 restoration

- **Frameworks that justify**:
  - Kaplan & Kaplan, Attention Restoration Theory (1989): four components — *being away*, *extent*, *fascination*, *compatibility* — and soft fascination as the mechanism.
  - Roger S. Ulrich, "View through a window may influence recovery from surgery," *Science* 224 (1984): 420–421 — the *physiological* correlate of restoration (shorter hospital stays, fewer complications).
  - Gehl, "soft edge" criterion: a place that lets you *opt in and out* of engagement is restorative in a way a hard place is not.
- **Verdict**: Strong. This is the dimension with the deepest empirical evidence in environmental psychology. No gap.

### C.5 independence

- **Frameworks that justify**:
  - Mayer Hillman, John Adams, John Whitelegg, *One False Move…* (PSI, 1990) — the foundational data on the collapse of children's independent mobility in the UK.
  - Leila Karsten, "It all used to be better?" *Children's Geographies* (2005) — the Dutch contrast that shows the collapse is not inevitable.
  - Gehl, "12 quality criteria" (2010/2017) — *protection, comfort, enjoyment* as the three preconditions for a child to be willing to act independently. If any of these fails, the child stays close to the adult.
- **Gap**: The dimension would benefit from also citing the *developmental* literature on *children's geographies* (e.g., the work of Karen Malone and Carmen Pérez del Pulgar) that links independent mobility to *risk assessment, spatial cognition, and resilience* in adolescence. These are the developmental mechanisms, not just the Gehl prerequisites.
- **Verdict**: Strong, with one missing developmental citation.

### C.6 adventure

- **Frameworks that justify**:
  - Ellen Beate Hansen Sandseter, six categories of risky play (2009 thesis; 2015 *ECEC* article): great heights, high speed, dangerous tools, dangerous elements, rough-and-tumble, disappear/get lost.
  - Mariana Brussoni et al., good risk vs bad risk (PubMed 26062038, 2015) — the *empirical* case that *overprotection* (the elimination of all risk) is itself a developmental harm.
  - Simon Nicholson, Theory of Loose Parts (1971) — the materials prerequisite for adventure (a place with fixed equipment has fewer adventure affordances than a place with loose parts).
  - Alexander #83 *Mastery* (1977) — the principle that a child needs a *gradient* of challenge to develop competence, and the adult's role is to *raise the gradient as the child grows*.
- **Verdict**: Strong. The four citations cover the *what* (Sandseter), the *why* (Brussoni), the *with-what* (Nicholson), and the *form* (Alexander). Adventure is the most *multiply-cited* dimension; this is correct because adventure is the most contested in contemporary parenting.

### C.7 Cross-dimension observation

The six dimensions are *collectively* an excellent match to the canon. They cover: belonging (Jacobs + Alexander + Sampson), nearby-nature (Louv + Kellert + Wolf), wonder (Kaplan + Tishman + Berleant), restoration (Kaplan ART + Ulrich + Gehl), independence (Hillman + Karsten + Gehl), adventure (Sandseter + Brussoni + Nicholson + Alexander). The Reset has a rare coherence: the dimensions don't double-cover, and the gaps in one dimension are filled by the strengths of another.

## D. Where the Reset design is WEAK — honest assessment

I want to be precise about which weaknesses are *gaps* (the canon says the slot should do more) versus which are *tensions* (the brief says something the canon does not).

### D.1 Gaps (canon says more)

1. **Arrival is inward; the canon says arrival is also outward.** The brief's arrival move is "kids go to the backyard." Jacobs, Allan Jacobs, and Alexander #165 all say the building edge should *also* open to the street. The Reset's arrival is missing the *neighbor* dimension.
2. **Change is logistics; the canon says change is ritual.** The brief's change move is "move the shoes." Alexander #61, #179, and Gehl's soft edges all say the threshold is an *architectural room* with its own affordances, not a logistics zone.
3. **Play is access; the canon says play is materials.** The brief's play move is "where are the scooters." Nicholson's loose parts, Sandseter's six categories, and Alexander #83 all say play needs *variable materials* in *multiple categories* of risk, not just one fixed affordance accessible without permission.
4. **Boundary is a rule; the canon says boundary is a place.** The brief's boundary move is "no screens until after dinner." Gehl's "soft edge" and Whyte's "triangulation" both say the boundary should also be a *physical, place-defining intervention* that *invites* a behavior, not a *clock-based prohibition* of one.
5. **Mom is one adult; the canon says Mom is a cluster.** The brief's "Mom" slot names one caregiver. Alexander #37, #38 and Hartz-Karp's deliberative-design work all say the family is a *cluster* of adults, and the *children themselves* are part of the cluster. The brief underdesigns the role of partner, neighbor, grandparent, and the children themselves.

### D.2 Tensions (brief says something the canon does not)

1. **"Mom" is gendered.** The canon (Alexander #38 *Marriage Family*, Hartz-Karp, UNICEF CFCI) is *explicit* that caregivers are plural and ungendered. The brief names one caregiver in a way that excludes partners, co-parents, and non-mother primary caregivers. This is the brief's *only* place where it visibly departs from the canon.
2. **The Reset is prescriptive; the canon is emergent.** Alexander's whole argument in *Pattern Language* is that patterns *emerge* from use. The Reset's move library of 30–50 moves (in `moves.js`) is closer to a *behavioral prescription* than to Alexander's user-generated pattern language. The Reset would be stronger if at least some moves came from *the family's own observations* of their week (a Whyte-style self-study), not just from a curated library.
3. **The 5-slot schema is complete at one window. The canon says the *next* window should re-pick its own slots.** Q4 ("the Next One") asks the family to pick the next hardest thing, but the brief does not say whether the 5-slot schema is *the* schema or *a* schema. Alexander's piecemeal growth principle (Pattern #1 *Independent Regions*, #2 *The Distribution of Towns*, and the opening pages of Part 1) is that each region *generates its own* pattern language, not that a single pattern language covers all regions. A canon-aligned Q4 would say: the *next* window may not have all five slots, and the family gets to discover which slots it needs.
4. **The brief treats the year as a year of *structural change* (rearranging the home). The canon (especially Janette Sadik-Khan's *Streetfight*, 2016) treats change as a series of *tactical pilots* — paint, bollards, a weekend, evaluate, swap. The Reset's moves are "low effort, reversible" by design, which is *exactly* the tactical-urbanism logic, but the brief does not call this out. This is a missed connection to a strong canon ally.

### D.3 Things the canon does not support

1. **The word "Reset."** The canon has no pattern named "reset" for family life. The closest analogs are Alexander #104 *Site Repair* (build on what already exists, don't bulldoze and start over) and Prochaska & DiClemente's *maintenance* stage (a stable pattern of behavior, not a periodic re-do). Calling the offering a "Reset" is a marketing word; the canon's word would be *repair*, *gradual stiffening*, or *piecemeal growth*.
2. **"Remove 3–5 decisions" as the unit of success.** This is *rational* (it's how a UX designer would describe the win) but it is not how the canon describes success. Gehl would say "make the outside choice the obvious choice." Jacobs would say "let the sidewalk ballet happen." Alexander would say "let the pattern strengthen through use." All three are softer and slower than "we removed three decisions." This is a tone question, not a correctness question.
3. **"Mom completes a 10–15 minute assessment" as the *intake* act.** Prochaska & DiClemente would say the parent may be in *precontemplation* or *contemplation* (the parent doesn't yet have a fully formed intention to change). A 10–15 minute assessment assumes the parent is already in *preparation* or *action*. The Reset's intake is missing a *readiness* question.

## E. The 4 quarters — verified against planning cadence

The Reset is structured as Q1 Hard Window → Q2 Neighborhood → Q3 Rhythm → Q4 Next One. The canon has direct support for each, with one quarter stronger than the others.

### E.1 Q1 — The Hard Window

- **Verified against**:
  - Gehl, *Cities for People* (2010): "first things first" — start with the *life* between buildings, not the buildings. The Hard Window is the family's "life between buildings" problem.
  - Alexander, *The Timeless Way of Building* (1979) and Pattern #1: each act of building should be *diffuse* (everyday) rather than *sharp* (a grand project). The Q1 Hard Window is *diffuse* — it picks the everyday, not the exceptional.
  - BJ Fogg, *Tiny Habits* (2019): anchor + tiny behavior + celebration. The Q1 Hard Window is essentially the *anchor* (the 4:30–7:30 transition) for the whole year.
- **Verdict**: Strong. Q1 is well-canonized as a *start where the pain is* pattern, which is also Sadik-Khan's tactical-urbanism logic — paint the plaza where pedestrians *already* are, not where you wish they were.

### E.2 Q2 — The Neighborhood

- **Verified against**:
  - Jacobs, "mixed primary uses" (one of the four generators of diversity, *Death and Life*, ch. 8): a neighborhood needs more than one use at more than one hour of day.
  - PPS, "Power of 10+" (PPS article, 2016): a place needs 10+ things to do; a neighborhood needs 10+ such places within walking distance.
  - Allan Jacobs, *Great Streets* (1993): a great street has identifiable physical characteristics that make it *legible* and *used*.
  - Oldenburg, *The Great Good Place* (1989) — implicit in the brief's "one regular third place" framing.
- **Gap (the canon says the quarter is missing the *who*)**: PPS's "Power of 10+" is not just 10 things; it is 10 things *that draw other people*. The "What attracts people most is other people" line (PPS) is the principle. The brief's Q2 is silent on *how* the third place is populated. The Q2 should include a "regulars" requirement, not just a "place" requirement.
- **Verdict**: Strong in structure, missing the *people* element.

### E.3 Q3 — The Rhythm

- **Verified against**:
  - Appleyard, *Livable Streets* (1981) and *Livable Streets 2.0* (2020): consistency over time is the difference between a livable street and a tolerable one. A street that is *predictable* and *recognizable* across the seasons is a street that people use.
  - Lally et al. (2010) — the *automaticity* literature. After 66 days (median) a behavior becomes relatively automatic; Q3 is the quarter where the family's moves should be reaching automaticity.
  - Prochaska & DiClemente: Q3 is the *maintenance* stage. This is exactly what the stage-of-change model says happens 3–6 months after action begins.
- **Gap (the canon says Q3 is missing observation)**: Whyte's *Social Life of Small Urban Spaces* is fundamentally a *method* book — the lesson is *go watch what people do*. The brief's Q3 is about *holding the pattern* across seasons, but it should also include a *self-observation* week, the way a Whyte-trained planner would do a *behavioral audit* of the family. "Watch yourself for a week" is the Whyte move that the Reset is missing.
- **Verdict**: Weakest quarter canonically. It has the behavioral-science support (Lally, Prochaska & DiClemente) but is thin on the urban-design canon. The fix is to add a *self-observation* week to Q3.

### E.4 Q4 — The Next One

- **Verified against**:
  - Alexander, *The Timeless Way of Building* (1979), opening chapters: *piecemeal growth*. A town that grows one building at a time, each by the people who will use it, is healthier than a town that grows by master plan.
  - Alexander #1 *Independent Regions*, #2 *The Distribution of Towns*, #3 *City Country Fingers*: each region solves its own problems at its own scale.
  - Janette Sadik-Khan, *Streetfight* (2016): the *cumulative* logic of tactical urbanism. You do one plaza, then another, then another. Each one teaches you something that informs the next.
- **Verdict**: Strong. Q4 is the *piecemeal growth* quarter, and it is canonically correct that the family picks the next window rather than the system prescribing it. This is also where Hartz-Karp's deliberative-design principle applies most directly: the family is now *co-designing* the next iteration.

### E.5 Cross-quarter observation

The four quarters are *collectively* an *incremental / piecemeal growth* structure, which is the strongest single principle in Alexander's *Timeless Way*. The Reset is, structurally, a year of piecemeal growth at the scale of family routine. This is the single strongest *canon-shaped* thing about the Reset and the parent should call it out in the marketing.

## F. The 1-year commitment — verified against behavioral design

A year is a long commitment. The canon says the following about sustaining change over a year.

### F.1 Lally et al. (2010) — habit formation is *slower* than 21 days

- Lally et al., "How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world," *European Journal of Social Psychology* 40 (2010): 998–1009.
- Median: 66 days to automaticity. Range: 18 to 254 days.
- A *year* is therefore *well past* the point of habit formation for most people on most behaviors.
- **Implication for the Reset**: the 1-year framing is correct. The 21-day myth (which traces to Maxwell Maltz's 1960 *Psycho-Cybernetics*, not to a habit-formation study) would be the wrong cadence. The 1-year commitment is *consistent* with the empirical literature.

### F.2 Prochaska & DiClemente (1983+) — stages of change

- Precontemplation → Contemplation → Preparation → Action → Maintenance → Termination.
- The Reset is mostly in the *Action → Maintenance* arc.
- **Gap (the canon says the intake should assess readiness)**: a 10–15 minute assessment that does not include a *readiness ruler* (e.g., "On a scale of 1–10, how ready are you to change this window?") skips Precontemplation and Contemplation. The brief should add a *readiness* signal that, when low, returns the parent to a *contemplation* path (e.g., "here are 3 small things you can try for a week before you commit").
- The Reset's "monthly is for testing the rhythm before committing" already partially covers this; the *annual* version could double down on it by requiring a *readiness check* at signup.

### F.3 BJ Fogg, *Tiny Habits* (2019) — anchor + tiny behavior + instant celebration

- The model: pick an existing *anchor* (a routine that already happens); attach a *tiny* version of the new behavior; *celebrate instantly* (even internally) to wire the positive emotion.
- **What the Reset has**: anchors (the 4:30–7:30 transition, the Saturday morning).
- **What the Reset is missing**: the *tiny* part. The current moves are sometimes *not* tiny ("move shoes, jackets, and outdoor clothes from the bedroom closets to the garage entry" is a weekend project, not a 30-second behavior). The Reset would be stronger if the *initial* move in each slot was a 30-second version of the larger move.
- **What the Reset is also missing**: the *instant celebration*. The "end-of-quarter milestone" is too far from the daily behavior to do the Fogg wiring. The Reset needs a *daily* or *per-occurrence* celebration, even if it's just a 3-second internal "yes" from the parent.
- **Verdict**: The Reset is structurally close to Fogg's model but underweights the *tiny* and *celebration* dimensions. Adding a 30-second version of each move and a daily celebration mechanic would tighten it to the Fogg canon.

### F.4 The quarterly cadence vs. daily streaks

- The Reset uses 4 quarterly checkpoints (one per quarter) + 3 monthly check-ins per quarter = 12 monthly touchpoints/year + 4 quarterly milestones + the moves themselves.
- This is closer to *maintenance-phase* behavioral design (Lally, Prochaska & DiClemente maintenance) than to *action-phase* (streak-based) design.
- The Lally literature suggests daily practice is the *formative* mechanism; the Prochaska & DiClemente literature suggests *relapse prevention* is the maintenance mechanism. The Reset's monthly + quarterly cadence is *correct* for the *maintenance* quarter (Q3) and *probably too sparse* for the *formative* quarter (Q1).
- **Verdict**: Right cadence for Q2–Q4. Q1 should add a *weekly* touchpoint in the first 6 weeks (the Lally median) to ensure the moves reach automaticity.

### F.5 Sustaining a year

- The Lally range is 18–254 days. The Reset is 365 days. The *back half* of the year is therefore *not* about forming the habit (that should have happened by Q2 or Q3) — it is about *maintaining, refreshing, and expanding* the pattern. The Q4 *Next One* is exactly the right move for this phase; the brief is well-aligned with the maintenance literature here.
- The biggest single risk across a year is *relapse* (Prochaska & DiClemente). The Reset's monthly check-ins are well-positioned to catch relapse, but the brief does not name "relapse" or specify the *swap-a-move* mechanic as a relapse response. It would be stronger if "swap a move" was framed as "this is what to do when a move has stopped working — the same move library serves you as a *swap* library."

## G. Specific recommendations — 5 concrete changes to tighten the Reset to the canon

Each recommendation cites the source. Each is concrete enough to land in the brief or the moves library.

### Recommendation 1 — Add a *neighbor-visibility* sub-move to the Arrival slot

**Source**: Jacobs, "eyes on the street" (*Death and Life*, 1961, ch. 3); Sampson, Raudenbush & Earls, *Science* 277 (1997): 918–924; Alexander #165 *Openness to the Street* (1977).

**The change**: pair the current "kids go directly to the backyard" move with a secondary move that opens the family's arrival to *being seen by a neighbor*. Examples: a move that names a specific neighbor ("knock on Mrs. __'s door on the way in, wave to the regular dog-walker"), a move that puts a *visible* element at the front of the house (a chalk drawing on the front walk, a chair on the porch), or a move that *routes* the kids past a window a neighbor can see into. The arrival becomes both private (the backyard) and public (a recognizable face on the block).

**Why this matters**: Sampson 1997 is the *empirical* proof that visibility predicts child wellbeing. The current arrival move is correct for the family but silent on the *neighborhood* effect that is the whole point of the word "belonging" in the Wilder dimensions.

### Recommendation 2 — Reframe the Play slot around *Nicholson's loose parts*, not around access to existing things

**Source**: Simon Nicholson, "How NOT to Cheat Children — The Theory of Loose Parts," *Landscape Architecture* (July 1971); Sandseter, six categories of risky play (2009/2015); Alexander #83 *Mastery* (1977).

**The change**: replace "scooters where kids can access them" with a *materials inventory* in the family intake. The moves library should include specific loose-parts moves ("a bin of sticks and boards by the back door," "a tarp and some clamps," "a 'kit' of cardboard boxes that gets rotated every two weeks," "a water table in the warm months"). Each move should target a *specific* Sandseter category (great heights / high speed / dangerous tools / dangerous elements / rough-and-tumble / disappear-get-lost) and should be marked as *variable* (the same materials support multiple play types).

**Why this matters**: Nicholson's principle is that the *number of variables* in the environment is what drives invention, not the *number of fixed affordances*. A scooter is a single variable (one affordance, one use). A bin of sticks is many variables. The Play slot is the single weakest slot canonically (see B.3) and this is the fix.

### Recommendation 3 — Give the Boundary slot a *physical, place-defining* element, not just a rule

**Source**: Gehl's "soft edge" criterion (*Cities for People*, 2010); Whyte, *Social Life of Small Urban Spaces* (1980), triangulation; Alexander #179 *Alcoves* and #180 *Window Place* (1977); Brussoni et al., "good risk vs bad risk," PubMed 26062038 (2015).

**The change**: every Boundary move should have both a *temporal* component (when the rule applies) and a *spatial* component (where the rule lives). Examples: "screens charge in the kitchen drawer, not by the couch" (a place, not just a rule); "the after-dinner hour happens on the porch, not at the kitchen table" (a place); "the screen-hour starts when the porch light is on" (a *signal* that is a place, not a clock). Each Boundary move should also have a *positive invitation* (the thing that *is* available at this hour) alongside the *prohibition* (the thing that is not). This converts a forbidding boundary into an inviting one, per Brussoni's good-risk logic.

**Why this matters**: Gehl's soft-edge criterion is that the *edge* of an activity should be *permeable* — the boundary should make it easy to do the right thing, not hard to do the wrong thing. The brief's current "screens don't become available until after dinner" is a hard, forbidding boundary; the canon's would be a *spatial* boundary that *invites* the alternate behavior.

### Recommendation 4 — Expand the Mom slot to *Mom + the cluster*, and add a *child-voice* sub-move

**Source**: Alexander #37 *House Cluster* and #38 *Marriage Family* (1977); Hartz-Karp, "Putting Children at the Center" and related deliberative-design work (2009, MDPI 2020); UNICEF Child Friendly Cities Initiative, "Voice" and "Participation" domains.

**The change**: the slot should be renamed to "Care" (or "Cluster") and should include a default *other-adults* move (partner, co-parent, neighbor, grandparent, regular sitter) and a default *child-voice* move (a weekly 5-minute family huddle where the kids name what worked and what didn't). The slot's "why" should change from "the adult's role" to "the cluster's role" — the family's small social contract.

**Why this matters**: Alexander's House Cluster is *not* one adult doing the cognitive work. It is *multiple* adults, multiple households, with overlapping obligations. The Reset's "Mom" framing is the *one* place where the brief visibly departs from the canon's universal caregiver language. This is also where Hartz-Karp's deliberative-design work applies most directly: the family, including the children, is *co-designing* the routine, not just receiving it from the system.

### Recommendation 5 — Add a *weekly self-observation* week to Q3 (Rhythm) and a *daily celebration* mechanic across all quarters

**Source**: William H. Whyte, *The Social Life of Small Urban Spaces* (1980) — the observation method; BJ Fogg, *Tiny Habits* (2019) — anchor + tiny behavior + instant celebration; Lally et al. (2010) — the formative window for automaticity is 18–254 days; Prochaska & DiClemente — maintenance is *active*, not passive.

**The change**: 
- In Q1, add a *weekly touchpoint* in the first 6 weeks (Lally's median) — a 30-second check-in the family can do on Sunday evening ("did the moves hold this week? swap one?"). This is the formative maintenance the Lally literature calls for.
- In Q3, add a *one-week self-observation* week. The family picks one week, does not change anything, and just *watches* the 4:30–7:30 transition (or whichever window). The Wilder app gives them a 5-prompt Whyte-style observation form: "When did the kids get outside? When did they get stuck? What did you see that surprised you?" The family's own observations become the input to the Q4 *Next One* decision.
- Across all quarters, add a *daily celebration* mechanic. A 3-second "yes" (per Fogg) when a move lands. The app could record these as a *streak of celebrations*, not a streak of completions — a fundamentally different thing, and the right one per Fogg.

**Why this matters**: Whyte's lesson is that *observation* is the foundation of good design; the Reset is currently moving from *intake* directly to *redesign* without an observation step. Fogg's lesson is that *celebration* is what wires a new behavior; the Reset is currently treating moves as *completed* (a boolean) rather than *celebrated* (an emotional event). Both are clean canon-aligned additions.

## H. What the canon does not say

Three things the brief assumes that the canon does not, strictly, support:

1. **The five-slot schema is canonical for family routine redesign.** The canon has *patterns* (Alexander) and *quality criteria* (Gehl) and *generators* (Jacobs) but it does not have a *prescriptive five-slot family-routine schema*. The Reset is building a new schema by *cross-cutting* these canons. The schema is a *legitimate* synthesis, not a direct citation. Call it what it is in the brief.
2. **A 1-year commitment is the right cadence.** Lally's 18–254 day range and Prochaska & DiClemente's stage model both support a year-long arc, but neither *prescribes* it. The 1-year commitment is a *product* decision dressed in behavioral-science language. The canon's actual answer is "as long as the maintenance phase lasts," which is open-ended.
3. **"Mom" as the primary caregiver.** The canon (Alexander, Hartz-Karp, UNICEF) is *explicitly* ungendered. The Reset's use of "Mom" is a *marketing* choice, not a *canon* choice. The brief should either justify the choice ("our customer is the primary caregiver, who is statistically more often the mother — see X") or replace the slot name with a universal one ("Care" or "Cluster").

## I. Summary

The Reset's design is *well-aligned* with the canon at the level of *principle* and *order of quarters*. It is *under-aligned* with the canon at the level of *second-order detail* — specifically, the neighbor dimension of Arrival, the materials dimension of Play, the place dimension of Boundary, the other-adults dimension of Mom, and the celebration/observation dimension across the year. The five concrete recommendations in Section G close those gaps without changing the Reset's overall structure. The Q3 *Rhythm* quarter is the weakest quarter canonically and benefits most from a Whyte-style self-observation week. The "Mom" framing is the one place where the brief visibly departs from the canon's universal caregiver language and should be reconsidered.
