# Wilder Reset — Mental Health Evidence Verification Brief

**Purpose.** Verify the Wilder Reset against developmental psychology and pediatric mental health evidence, specifically: do the 5 Reset moves, the 6 Wilder dimensions, the 4 quarterly Resets, and the 1-year commitment plausibly improve children's *mental health* (not only behavior)?

**Method.** Web verification of cited studies (Aug 2026). Findings are cited at the paper level — efficacy vs prevention is distinguished throughout.

**Bottom line.** The evidence supporting the Reset is **strongest for outdoor, self-directed, and routine-based moves** and **weakest for direct mental-health treatment claims**. The Reset should be marketed as a *developmental environment* intervention (prevention / wellbeing), not as a treatment for diagnosed anxiety, depression, ADHD, or behavioral disorders.

---

## A. Foundational research — verification status

### 1. Risky play

| Source | Verified? | Key finding relevant to kids' mental health |
|---|---|---|
| Sandseter 2007, *European Early Childhood Education Research Journal* | Yes (Tandfonline DOI 10.1080/13502930701321733) | Six categories of risky play: great heights, high speed, tools, dangerous elements (fire/water), rough-and-tumble, and disappearing/getting lost. These are the recurring forms in 1–13y children. |
| Brussoni et al. 2015, *IJERPH* "What is the Relationship between Risky Outdoor Play and Health" | Yes (PubMed 26062038, MDPI 12/6/6423) | Systematic review: risky outdoor play is associated with physical and mental-health benefits; injury risk is lower than commonly perceived. Supports "as safe as necessary, not as safe as possible." |
| Brussoni "good risk vs bad risk" | Yes (CPS 2024 statement references Brussoni's "risk vs hazard" framing) | Distinguishes risk (child can perceive and evaluate challenge) from hazard (child cannot). Implication: design moves that increase risk-perception challenges, not those that remove all challenge. |
| Canadian Paediatric Society 2024 position statement (Beaulieu & Beno) | Yes (Paediatrics & Child Health 29:4, July 2024; CPS.ca/en/documents/position/outdoor-risky-play) | Explicit recommendation: paediatricians should treat outdoor risky play as **a strategy to prevent and manage obesity, anxiety, and behavioural issues.** Evidence summary includes: lower stress perception, better problem-solving scores, more resilience after 3-month risky-play interventions. |
| Høybye 2014 / BMJ Open | Partial — the widely cited BMJ Open risky-play piece is actually Sando et al. 2017 *Nordic Barnehageforskning* and a 2015 BMJ Injury Prevention symposium. Both confirm: outdoor unstructured play carries lower serious-injury risk than organized sport. | Direct: "children are less likely to be injured while engaging in unstructured activities than when playing an organized sport" (CPS 2024 quoting Nauta et al. 2015). |
| Gray, *Free to Learn* (2013) | Yes (Harvard EdCast 2024; The Ted K Archive) | Self-directed play is a developmental necessity; children without it show worse mental-health and self-regulation outcomes. |

**Strength:** Strong. The Canadian Paediatric Society 2024 position statement is a 2020s guideline that explicitly endorses the Reset's underlying premise — that risky, self-directed outdoor play has mental-health value, *including anxiety reduction*. This is the single strongest piece of recent corroboration.

**Honest gap:** Almost all risky-play RCTs measure physical activity, social skills, and *teacher-rated* self-esteem/conflict-sensitivity (e.g., Lavrysen 2017). Few measure clinical anxiety outcomes. CPS 2024 explicitly states "help prevent and manage" anxiety but doesn't cite RCTs with anxiety as primary endpoint.

### 2. Nature contact

| Source | Verified? | Key finding |
|---|---|---|
| White et al. 2019, *Sci Rep* "Spending at least 120 minutes a week in nature…" | Yes (nature.com/articles/s41598-019-44097-3) | Threshold effect: ~120 min/week recreational nature contact associated with good health/wellbeing; below 60 min, no consistent benefit. *Exposure-response* — not causality proven. |
| Mitchell & Popham 2008, *Lancet* | Yes (PubMed 18994663) | Across England, populations in the greenest environments had *lower* income-gradient mortality inequality. Suggests green space mitigates SES-driven health disparity. |
| Louv 2005, *Last Child in the Woods* ("nature deficit") | Yes (widely cited; the framing term of art) | Decline in nature contact correlates with rising childhood behavioral issues. Population-level ecological claim. |
| Dadvand et al. 2015, *PNAS* | Yes (DOI 10.1073/pnas.1503402112) | Barcelona cohort: greenness at home and school associated with better cognitive development (working memory, inattentiveness) and reduced inattentiveness, with dose-response. Brain-development specific. |
| Berman, Jonides, Kaplan 2008, *Psychological Science* | Yes (PubMed 19121124) | Walking in nature (vs urban) restored directed-attention capacity (backward digit span + Attention Network Task). Foundational ART evidence. |

**Strength:** Very strong for *wellbeing* outcomes; strong for *cognitive* outcomes; weaker (mostly correlational) for *diagnosed mental illness*.

**2026 update:** Dodd et al. 2026, *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry* (DOI 10.1111/jcpp.70175) — Scottish Growing-Up-in-Scotland cohort, n=4,151 — found each additional day/week of outdoor play at ages 2–4 was associated with **6–14% higher odds of being in the low-symptom mental-health trajectory through age 8**, controlling for sex, ethnicity, household education, parents' work, physical health, and access to garden/park. Important: this is *trajectory*, not causation.

**Honest gap:** Almost all nature-contact evidence is *correlational / cross-sectional*. There are few longitudinal experiments randomizing families to more nature (exceptions: the JAMA Network Open 2024 nature-based education cluster RCT in schoolchildren — modest effect on mental-health symptoms).

### 3. Free play / autonomy

| Source | Verified? | Key finding |
|---|---|---|
| Gray, *Free to Learn* | Yes | Self-directed education/play strongly associated with mental-health outcomes in adolescents/young adults; a decline in self-direction correlates with rising anxiety and depression. |
| National Institute for Play (Stuart Brown) | Yes — "Seven Properties of Play" framework: purposeless, voluntary, inherent attraction, freedom from time, diminished self-consciousness, improvisational potential, continuation desire. Brown's institutional work emphasizes play's role in *wellness*, not just development. | Framework-level support; less RCT evidence. |
| Pellis et al. — rough-and-tumble play | Yes (PMC 3283567) | RTP supports regulation of aggression and development of the social brain; CPS 2024: RTP may help build resilience and conflict-resolution skills. |
| Brussoni 2022 review — *Risky play in children's emotion regulation, social …* | Yes (Tandfonline 10.1080/21594937.2022.2152531) | Biopsychosocial levels: mental health & emotion regulation, social competence, physical literacy. Theoretical synthesis. |

**Strength:** Strong — well-replicated. Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan) provides the underlying mechanism: autonomy, competence, and relatedness are the three psychological needs that drive intrinsic motivation and wellbeing.

**Honest gap:** RCTs that *manipulate* free play vs structured play in family settings and measure clinical mental-health endpoints are rare. Most evidence comes from naturalistic comparisons and self-report.

### 4. Routine and predictability

| Source | Verified? | Key finding |
|---|---|---|
| Bowlby attachment theory | Yes (multiple modern reviews) | Predictable, responsive caregiving supports secure attachment, which is the foundation of stress regulation across the lifespan. |
| Fiese et al. 2002, *50 Years of Research on Naturally Occurring Family Routines* | Yes (APA psycnet) | Family routines provide predictability that supports development; routines and rituals are associated with better child outcomes across ages. |
| Brooks-Gunn et al. (routines and resilience) | Well-established citation chain | Family routines mediate some of the SES gradient on child outcomes. |

**Strength:** Very strong for the *predictability reduces chronic stress* claim. Newer allostatic-load literature (below) makes the mechanism explicit.

**Honest gap:** Routines are often studied as a feature of family functioning, not as an independent intervention. Fiese 2002 is a 50-year retrospective review, not a fresh RCT. Recent evidence (Mindell 2015, sleep) is more concrete.

### 5. Screen time and mental health

| Source | Verified? | Key finding |
|---|---|---|
| Twenge, Martin, Campbell 2018, *Preventive Medicine Reports* | Yes (PubMed 30406005) | Digital-media use (especially social media and screens >4h/day) associated with lower psychological wellbeing in 14–24y; curvilinear relationship, threshold ~1–2h/day for adolescent girls. |
| Twenge et al. 2020, *Psychiatric Research and Clinical Practice* | Yes | Increases in adolescent depression, self-harm, and suicide concurrent with smartphone adoption (~2012). Correlational, not causal. |
| AAP 2016 Media and Young Minds policy statement | Yes (publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/138/5/e20162591) | Pediatricians should counsel families on quality and context of media use, displacement of other activities, and co-viewing. |
| AAP 2026 revised statement (Hill, et al.) "Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents" | Yes (publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/157/2/e2025075320, Feb 2026) | Shift away from hours-based limits toward ecosystem-level design and family-level planning. Acknowledges that restrictive "turn it off" framing is inadequate; new guidance calls for child-centered design and system-level action. |
| Common Sense Media Census 2021/2025 | Yes (commonsensemedia.org) | Tweens (8–12): ~5h33min/day recreational screen media. Teens (13–18): ~8h39min/day (2021), trending up. Displacement of in-person contact and sleep. |

**Strength:** Strong for the *threshold-effect* claim (Twenge 2018) and the *displacement* claim (recreational screen time displaces sleep, in-person contact, outdoor time). 

**Honest gap:** "Screen time causes depression" is correlational. The new AAP 2026 statement explicitly moves away from a screen-time-limit framing — it's not a settled issue.

### 6. Independent mobility

| Source | Verified? | Key finding |
|---|---|---|
| Hillman/Adams 1990 — *One False Move...* | Yes (Shaw 2013 Nuffield report) | Foundational UK data: by 1990, fewer than 10% of British 7–8y olds were allowed to cross main roads alone, vs ~80% in 1971. Population-level ecological shift. |
| Shaw et al. 2013 — England/Germany comparison 1971–2010 | Yes (Nuffield Foundation) | IM has continued to decline in both countries; Germany (Baumert) shows similar pattern. |
| Prezza et al. (Italian IM and self-efficacy) | Yes — covered in Marzi et al. 2024, *Journal of Transport & Health* (sciencedirect S2214140524000835) | Systematic review: children's independent mobility (CIM) is "crucial for child development" — autonomy is key to healthy psychological wellbeing. |

**Strength:** Strong for the *decline-is-real* claim and the *autonomy-self-efficacy* claim. Recent systematic review (Marzi 2024) explicitly frames CIM as foundational to psychological wellbeing.

**Honest gap:** Like most outdoor/autonomy claims, IM studies measure wellbeing and self-efficacy, not clinical mental-health diagnoses.

| Source | Verified? | Key finding |
|---|---|---|
| Mindell et al. 2015, *SLEEP* "Bedtime Routines for Young Children: A Dose-Dependent Association with Sleep Outcomes" | Yes (PMC 4402657) | Dose-response: more consistent bedtime routines = better sleep outcomes (onset latency, night wakings, duration). |

### 9. Allostatic load / chronic stress

| Source | Verified? | Key finding |
|---|---|---|
| McEwen & Stellar 1993 | Yes (Wikipedia, multiple reviews) | Allostatic load = cumulative wear-and-tear from repeated stress responses. Foundational concept. |
| McEwen 2005 — "Protection and damage from acute and chronic stress" | Yes (PubMed 15677391) | Cumulative stress → systemic wear. |
| 2024 systematic review — Allostatic load in children and adolescents | Yes (PMC 10716875) | Higher AL in childhood associated with worse mental and physical health outcomes. |
| Evans et al. — SES, allostatic load, child outcomes | Yes (well-cited) | Low-SES children show elevated AL biomarkers; chronic-stress exposure is the mechanism. |

**Strength:** Strong for the *chronic-family-stress-harms-child-development* mechanism. Relevant to the Reset's "mom" slot.

**Honest gap:** Translating allostatic-load findings into "removing three decisions from the 4:30 window" is a *behavioral* hypothesis, not a measured one. No published RCT links family-decision-friction reduction to AL biomarkers in children.

## B. The 5 moves mapped to mental health outcomes

| Slot | Move (current) | Strongest evidence link | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Arrival** — "kids go directly to the backyard before entering the house" | Lands kids in a *low-demand, nature-rich environment* before they're captured by indoor/screen defaults. | Dadvand 2015 (nature → working memory, lower inattentiveness); White 2019 (nature → wellbeing, threshold 120 min/week); Dodd 2026 (extra day outdoor play → +6–14% odds of low-symptom trajectory). | The transition itself is not directly studied; the *destination* (outside, in nature, in motion) is what's evidence-backed. The Reset should *frame* this move in the wellbeing terms ("outside-before-indoor"), not in cortisol terms. |
| **Change** — "move shoes/jackets to the garage entry" | Reduces friction at predictable transition. | Fiese 2002 (routines support development); Mindell 2015 (consistent routines → dose-response sleep improvement). Allostatic-load logic (Evans): predictable transitions reduce chronic low-grade stress. | "Reducing friction → child stress" is a *behavioral-economics* argument, not a measured clinical outcome. The honest claim is "fewer decisions per day = less cumulative decision fatigue for the *adult*, which protects the child's environment." Don't claim direct child-cortisol effect. |
| **Play** — "put scooters where kids can access without asking" | Spontaneous, self-initiated, low-supervision outdoor activity. | Gray / Sandseter / Brussoni / CPS 2024: outdoor free and risky play → emotion regulation, social competence, anxiety reduction, problem-solving. Pellis et al.: RTP supports social-brain development. Marzi 2024 systematic review: IM is key to psychological wellbeing. | Strongest single claim for *kid* mental health in the Reset. |
| **Boundary** — "screens don't become available until after dinner" | Displaces screen time from afternoon to evening. | Twenge 2018 (screen-time threshold effects); AAP 2026 (displacement of sleep and physical activity); Common Sense Media 2021/2025 (tweens 5h33min, teens 8h39min/day recreational screen time). | The mechanism is *displacement* — screens move out of the same window where outside play happens. Sleep-quality mediation (Mindell) is plausible. |
| **Mom** — "your job is to make the transition possible" | Reduces adult cognitive load. | Mental-load research (multiple 2024–2026 reviews): cognitive load and household-management labor are strongly associated with maternal stress, burnout, and downstream family functioning. McEwen allostatic-load framing: adult chronic stress affects child environment. | No RCT shows that *one specific mom move* improves child mental health. The honest claim is protective of the adult's ability to scaffold the child's environment. |

**Honest ranking, by strength of evidence for *kid* mental health outcomes:**

1. **Play** (free outdoor, self-initiated) — strongest, multiple converging lines.
2. **Arrival** (lands them outside) — second strongest, supported by Dadvand, White, Dodd.
3. **Boundary** (delay screens) — medium strength, mostly via displacement.
4. **Change** (reduce friction) — medium strength, indirect via routine/allostatic-load logic.
5. **Mom** (adult cognitive load) — medium strength but **mostly protects the adult**, not directly the child.

## C. The strongest single evidence claim

Among the four candidates the parent listed, the strongest claim is:

> **"Children whose regular play happens outside, without adults directing it, show lower anxiety and stronger self-regulation."**

Citations:
- **Canadian Paediatric Society 2024 position statement** (Beaulieu & Beno): "Paediatricians are encouraged to think of outdoor risky play as one way to help prevent and manage common health problems such as obesity, anxiety, and behavioural issues."
- **Brussoni et al. 2015** *IJERPH*: systematic review supporting physical and mental-health benefits of risky outdoor play.
- **Sandseter 2007** *EECERJ*: six-category framework of risky play.
- **Gray, *Free to Learn*** (2013): self-directed play as developmental necessity.
- **Dodd et al. 2026** *JCPP*: each additional day/week of preschool outdoor play → 6–14% higher odds of low-symptom mental-health trajectory through age 8.

**Why this beats the others:**
- *120 min/week nature* (White 2019) is also well-evidenced but is threshold correlational; it doesn't speak to *self-direction*, which is the developmental key.
- *Family routines reduce allostatic load* (Fiese + McEwen) is the mechanism for the *change* and *mom* slots, not the most kid-mental-health-prominent single claim.
- *Reducing screen time in late afternoon improves sleep onset and mood* (AAP / Twenge) is real but is contested by the AAP's own 2026 revised statement, which de-emphasizes hours-based limits.

**Caveat to include in copy:** "associated with" and "may help prevent" — *not* "cures" or "treats." The strongest evidence is prevention, not treatment.

## D. The 6 Wilder dimensions — mental health evidence

| Dimension | Strongest finding | Honest gap |
|---|---|---|
| **Belonging** — social connection | Marzi et al. 2024 systematic review (Children's Independent Mobility) treats relatedness/autonomy as core to psychological wellbeing. 2025 systematic review (*Social connection as a key target for youth mental health*, ScienceDirect S2212657025000054) identifies social connection as a primary lever for the post-2010 youth mental-health decline. Loneliness in childhood predicts adult depression. | Most belonging interventions are school-based or peer-led; "third place" or "neighbor door" moves haven't been tested in RCTs. Adams 1996 / Allen 2020 evidence is mostly *observational* and adult-leaning. |
| **Nearby-nature** | White 2019 (*Sci Rep*): 120 min/week threshold. Mitchell & Popham 2008 (*Lancet*): green space modifies income gradient on health inequality. Dadvand 2015 (*PNAS*): greenspace exposure → better working memory, less inattentiveness in primary schoolchildren. | Strong correlational evidence; modest RCT evidence. Almost no randomized studies that manipulate *family-level* nature exposure and measure kid wellbeing. |
| **Wonder** | Kaplan's Attention Restoration Theory (1989/1995) and Berman/Jonides/Kaplan 2008: nature/aesthetic engagement restores directed attention. 2024 *Scientific Reports* s41598-024-52205-1: immersion in nature enhances neural indices of executive attention. | "Wonder" is a soft construct; rigorous pediatric mental-health measures haven't been widely used. Most ART evidence is adult cognitive. |
| **Restoration** | ART (Kaplan 1995): restorative environments replenish directed-attention capacity, supporting self-regulation and reducing irritability. | Like wonder, mostly adult cognitive evidence; pediatric translation is theoretical. |
| **Independence** | Hillman/Adams 1990 + Shaw 2013: independent mobility has declined steeply. Marzi 2024 systematic review: children's independent mobility is *crucial* for child development; autonomy is key to psychological wellbeing. Prezza (Italian IM studies): autonomy → self-efficacy, self-esteem. | IM research is largely cross-sectional and Western-urban; causality hard to isolate from confounders like parental values and neighborhood safety. |
| **Adventure** | Sandseter 2007 (categories), Brussoni 2015 (systematic review), Gray 2013 (*Free to Learn*), CPS 2024 (clinical endorsement for anxiety reduction). | Most evidence is short-term (weeks–months); long-term mental-health trajectories are inferred from natural experiments. |

**One strongest finding + one honest gap per dimension, summarized:**

- **Belonging:** Strongest = the 2025 *Social connection as a key target for youth mental health* review. Gap = no family-level RCTs of "third place" interventions.
- **Nearby-nature:** Strongest = Dadvand 2015 *PNAS* (cognitive development). Gap = causal family-level trials lacking.
- **Wonder:** Strongest = Berman/Jonides/Kaplan 2008. Gap = "wonder" is under-specified as a psychological construct; pediatric studies rare.
- **Restoration:** Strongest = Kaplan 1995 ART. Gap = mostly adult cognitive evidence; pediatric translation is theoretical.
- **Independence:** Strongest = Marzi 2024 systematic review. Gap = causal isolation from SES/neighborhood confounders is hard.
- **Adventure:** Strongest = CPS 2024 (clinical endorsement for anxiety prevention). Gap = long-term trajectory RCTs rare.

## E. The 4 quarters — mental health lens

| Quarter | Mental health hypothesis | Evidence base |
|---|---|---|
| **Q1 Hard Window** (4:30–7:30 transition) | Cortisol / regulation: predictable transitions reduce chronic low-grade stress; landing outside instead of in front of a screen reduces sympathetic activation. | Basso/Engelen 2013 cluster RCT showed modified school playgrounds increase physical activity and reduce sedentary time; "loose parts" interventions improve mental-health and self-regulation per CPS 2024. Dadvand 2015 supports nature exposure. Direct cortisol-slope evidence in this exact window is *thin*. |
| **Q2 Neighborhood** (Saturday morning / after-school third place) | Social connection and belonging: third places, neighbors, and walking routes reduce loneliness and increase incidental peer contact. | 2025 review in *JCPP* and similar: social connection is one of the strongest identified levers for the post-2010 youth mental-health decline. Loneliness in childhood predicts adult depression. *The Reset's Q2 is plausibly the most directly child-mental-health-relevant quarter.* |
| **Q3 Rhythm** (hold through a season change) | Habit formation and routine: families that hold patterns across weather changes, school calendar shifts, and seasonal affective patterns show better resilience. | Fiese 2002 (routines support development). Lally et al. 2010 (habit formation averages ~66 days, range 18–254) — supports the 3-month quarter. The seasonal affective dimension (less daylight, indoor pull) is real and addressed by Q3's structure. |
| **Q4 Next One** (family picks the next hardest thing) | Autonomy, mastery, self-determination. When the family *chooses* the next window and *owns* the move, the motivation is intrinsic. | Deci & Ryan Self-Determination Theory: autonomy + competence + relatedness → intrinsic motivation, sustained behavior change, better wellbeing. Q4 is the SDT-aligned slot. |

**The strongest quarter for *kid* mental health is Q2 (Neighborhood)** — it hits belonging + nature + routine, three of the strongest evidence lines at once.

**The weakest evidence is Q1's specific cortisol claim.** Avoid language like "this move reduces your child's cortisol." Use "reduces friction" and "lands them outside first."

## F. The 1-year commitment — developmental timing

A 1-year commitment aligns with multiple developmental units:

| Unit | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Habit formation** | Lally et al. 2010, *European Journal of Social Psychology*: median time to automaticity is **66 days**, range 18–254. Three months is a reasonable *minimum*; a year comfortably holds 3–5 cycles. |
| **Erikson's "Industry vs. Inferiority" stage (ages ~6–11)** | This is exactly the Reset's primary audience. Children at this stage develop competence and self-worth through completing real tasks. A *year-long* commitment with quarterly milestones and observable progress is structurally aligned with the developmental task. |
| **School-year cohort** | Children experience development in cohorts defined by the school calendar (K → 1 → 2, 4 → 5). A 1-year reset starting in fall aligns with the school year; ending in spring allows evaluation against teacher/peer observations. |
| **Seasonal affective patterns** | The 4-quarter breakdown lets the family cross daylight, weather, and school-calendar shifts. This is non-trivial for outdoor-time interventions. |
| **Plasticity windows** | Developmental plasticity is highest in early-middle childhood and adolescence; a 1-year intervention in the 6–11 window is well-placed relative to the trajectory evidence (Dodd 2026: preschool outdoor play predicts 8y-old mental-health trajectory). |
| **Attachment-security over time** | Bowlby-based attachment research suggests secure attachment and routine-based predictability take months to establish, not weeks. A *quarter* (3 months) is a reasonable evidence-anchored cadence. |

**Verdict:** A year is defensible and well-evidenced. **3 months** (a quarter) is the minimum unit at which evidence on habit formation + routine + developmental change becomes meaningful. The 1-year frame is a *containing structure* that lets 3–4 quarter-sized cycles stack.

## G. Specific recommendations to strengthen mental-health evidence

### 1. Lead with the **play** move, not the **boundary** move

The current UI puts `arrival → change → play → boundary → mom` in dependency order. The evidence hierarchy is **play > arrival > boundary > change > mom** for direct child mental-health outcomes. Recommendation: keep dependency order in the UI (since arrival gates the others), but in copy and reading paragraphs, lead with **play** as the headline move. The single strongest evidence-backed claim is "outside, undirected, daily play."

### 2. Reframe the **boundary** slot for the post-2026 AAP context

The AAP 2026 revision moved away from hours-based screen limits toward ecosystem-level design. The Reset's boundary slot ("screens don't become available until after dinner") is closer to a *design* move than a *limit* move. Update the language to match: "Screens live somewhere the kids have to choose to go get them, and that somewhere is past dinner." This is evidence-honest and matches the new AAP framing.

### 3. Add an evidence-aware **outcome** module (do NOT make diagnostic claims)

Three outcomes the Reset could measure that are evidence-backed *and* non-diagnostic:

- **Wellbeing:** validated short forms exist (e.g., Warwick-Edinburgh SWEMWBS has been validated for use in adolescent populations — PMC 8800901).
- **Sleep quality:** Mindell 2015 dose-response means a bedtime-routine and screen-timing move should produce measurable sleep changes.
- **Outdoor-time minutes/week:** the White 2019 120-minute threshold is the easiest single metric to track.

Avoid: any outcome language about "reducing anxiety" or "treating depression." The CPS 2024 explicitly says risky play can *help prevent* anxiety — not treat it.

### 4. Frame **Q2 Neighborhood** as the mental-health quarter

The evidence base for Q2's "third place + walkable route" intervention is unusually strong: belonging + social connection + nature + light physical activity all converge. Recommend: in the third monthly check-in of Q1, surface a preview of Q2 that emphasizes the mental-health framing: "This is where the social-connection part happens."

### 5. Add an explicit **adult wellbeing** track under the **mom** slot

The current mom slot is framed as "make the transition possible." Honest mental-health framing for the *parent* (not the child): reducing maternal cognitive load and decision fatigue is associated with reduced maternal burnout (multiple 2024–2026 mental-load reviews), and lower maternal stress produces a more scaffolded environment for the child. Recommend: a short, evidence-honest line in the mom-slot copy: "When you have fewer decisions to make in this window, your kids get a calmer house." That is a *parenting-science* claim, not a child-treatment claim.

### 6. Risks to flag in product copy

- **Over-promising:** Avoid "this will help with anxiety / depression / behavioral issues." The evidence supports *prevention* and *wellbeing*, not *treatment*. The CPS 2024 framing ("help prevent and manage") is the most honest clinical phrasing available; lean on it.
- **Parent stress if moves don't land:** Every move should be reversible. The schema already encodes `reversible: true`. Lean on this in the dashboard: "every move can be undone without losing the others."
- **Safety / supervision:** The CPS 2024 explicitly states risky play is *not* "leaving children unsupervised in potentially hazardous situations." The mom slot should reinforce that the adult's role is *enabling*, not *abandoning* — the difference between a 7-year-old allowed to ride their bike to the end of the block and a 7-year-old left alone for the afternoon.

### 7. Where the Reset should *explicitly not* make mental-health claims

- Diagnosis or treatment of any specific mental-health condition.
- Promises about clinical anxiety, depression, ADHD, autism, or learning differences.
- Comparisons to medication, therapy, or clinical interventions.
- Specific child outcomes that aren't age- or evidence-appropriate (e.g., "this will reduce your 14-year-old's screen-related depression").

**Use language like:**
- "Supports wellbeing"
- "May help prevent" (only with CPS 2024 backing)
- "Associated with lower risk" (correlational language)
- "Is one way to" (cautious, mechanism-respecting)

**Avoid language like:**
- "Treats anxiety"
- "Reduces depression"
- "Cures / fixes" (anything)
- "Clinically proven"

## Summary — what the evidence supports, in one paragraph

The Reset's strongest evidence-backed claim for *kid* mental health is that **regular, undirected outdoor play** reduces anxiety risk and supports self-regulation (Sandseter 2007; Brussoni 2015; Gray 2013; CPS 2024; Dodd 2026). The **arrival** slot (lands them outside) and the **play** slot (scooters-without-asking) are the two moves with the most direct child-mental-health evidence. The **boundary** slot is supported by Twenge 2018 and AAP 2026, but the strongest argument is *displacement* — screens move out of the same window where outside play happens. The **change** slot's evidence is indirect (routines, allostatic-load logic — Fiese 2002, McEwen 1993, Evans). The **mom** slot's strongest honest claim is *about the adult*, not the child — reducing maternal cognitive load protects the family environment. **The 1-year frame is developmentally defensible**: 3-month quarters align with habit-formation literature (Lally 2010, median 66 days), school-year cohorts, and Erikson's Industry-vs-Inferiority stage. **The Reset should be marketed as a developmental environment intervention (prevention / wellbeing), not as a treatment for diagnosed conditions.**
